**UNVERIFIED — the original minimax problem remains unresolved.** The intermediate result below has a complete derivation, but has not been accepted by a verifier. It gives a concrete obstruction to projection-based adversaries and identifies precisely how a slightly richer geometric construction escapes that obstruction.

The result is an exact distinction:

- Arbitrary squared distances to convex sets, even allowing every dimension and curvature, have precisely the same worst-case power as scalar quadratics. Their optimal profile is \((2n+1)^{-2}\), which is too small to prove the requested lower bound.
- Squared distances to polyhedra with one coordinate held fixed are already sufficient to approximate every finite GD run, including its terminal objective. At horizon \(n\), at most \(n+2\) defining inequalities suffice. The approximation error below is explicit and accommodates arbitrary nonnegative steps.

The second statement is a representation theorem, not a constant-preserving adversary construction for the Zhang–Jiang benchmark.

Normalize \(L=1\), \(x_\star=0\), \(f_\star=0\), and \(\|x_0\|=1\). This loses no generality: for an original initial distance \(r>0\), replace \(f\) by
\[
F(z)=\frac{f(x_\star+rz)-f_\star}{Lr^2}.
\]
The normalized performance is then \(2F(z_n)\).

**UNVERIFIED proposition: ordinary squared-distance adversaries collapse exactly to quadratics.**

For every \(n\ge0\) and every \(h\in[0,\infty)^n\), consider the class
\[
f_{\lambda,K}(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\operatorname{dist}(x,K)^2,
\qquad 0<\lambda\le1,
\]
where \(K\) is any nonempty closed convex set in any finite dimension. Its normalized worst-case performance equals
\[
Q_n(h):=\max_{0\le\lambda\le1}
\lambda\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}(1-\lambda h_t)^2.
\tag{1}
\]
Moreover,
\[
\inf_{h\in[0,\infty)^n}Q_n(h)=\frac1{(2n+1)^2}.
\tag{2}
\]

To prove (1), let \(p_t=P_Kx_t\). Squared distance is convex and differentiable, with
\[
\nabla f_{\lambda,K}(x)=\lambda(x-P_Kx).
\]
Its gradient is \(\lambda\)-Lipschitz. These properties follow from the projection optimality inequality
\[
\langle x-P_Kx,z-P_Kx\rangle\le0\qquad(z\in K);
\]
applying it at two points shows that \(I-P_K\) is firmly nonexpansive.

The GD update is
\[
x_{t+1}=(1-\lambda h_t)x_t+\lambda h_t p_t.
\]
Since \(p_t\in K\), for every nonnegative step, including steps exceeding \(2/\lambda\),
\[
\operatorname{dist}(x_{t+1},K)
\le \|x_{t+1}-p_t\|
=|1-\lambda h_t|\operatorname{dist}(x_t,K).
\]
Consequently, for any \(x_\star\in K\),
\[
\frac{2f_{\lambda,K}(x_n)}{\|x_0-x_\star\|^2}
\le
\lambda\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}(1-\lambda h_t)^2.
\]
Equality is attained by \(K=\{0\}\) in dimension one. This proves (1), including the supremum over all convex sets.

Here is a self-contained proof of (2). Put \(m=2n+1\) and
\[
q(x)=x\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}(1-h_tx^2).
\]
Then \(q\) is odd, has degree at most \(m\), satisfies \(q'(0)=1\), and
\[
Q_n(h)=\|q\|_{\infty,[-1,1]}^2.
\]

Define
\[
q_\ast(x)=\frac{\sin(m\arcsin x)}m.
\]
For odd \(m\), this is an odd polynomial of degree \(m\), with derivative \(1\) at zero and supremum norm \(1/m\).

No odd polynomial \(q\) of degree at most \(m\), with \(q'(0)=1\), can have strictly smaller norm. Indeed, at the \(n+1\) positive points
\[
\xi_j=\sin\frac{(2j+1)\pi}{2m},
\qquad 0\le j\le n,
\]
we have
\[
q_\ast(\xi_j)=\frac{(-1)^j}{m}.
\]
If \(\|q\|_\infty<1/m\), the difference \(q-q_\ast\) changes sign between every consecutive pair of these points. It therefore has \(n\) positive roots and, by oddness, \(n\) negative roots. Its root at zero has multiplicity at least three because its derivative there vanishes. That gives at least \(2n+3\) roots counted with multiplicity, exceeding its degree.

Finally,
\[
q_\ast(x)
=x\prod_{j=1}^{n}
\left(1-\frac{x^2}{\sin^2(j\pi/m)}\right).
\]
Thus equality is attained by the nonnegative steps
\[
h_j=\csc^2\frac{j\pi}{2n+1},
\qquad 1\le j\le n,
\tag{3}
\]
in any order.

This supplies a precise obstruction. Writing \(B_n=2S_\bullet(n)+1\), the supplied growth estimate gives
\[
\frac{\inf_h Q_n(h)}{U_n^{\mathrm{ZJ}}}
=\frac{B_n}{(2n+1)^2}
=\Theta(n^{\rho-2})\longrightarrow0.
\tag{4}
\]
Therefore even this unrestricted collection of nonlinear squared-distance objectives cannot establish (Q). Equation (4) is **not** a full-class upper bound and gives no negative resolution of (P).

**UNVERIFIED proposition: fixing one extra coordinate makes squared-distance geometry universal for finite GD runs.**

For every fixed \(n\), every \(h\in[0,\infty)^n\), every normalized admissible GD run, and every \(\eta>0\), there exist:

- a dimension \(d\le n+1\);
- a finite convex function
  \[
  p(y)=\max\{0,\ell_0(y),\ldots,\ell_n(y)\},
  \qquad p(0)=0,
  \tag{5}
  \]
  with affine \(\ell_i\);
- a number \(R>0\);

such that the single static function
\[
F_R(x)
=
\frac12\operatorname{dist}\!\left((x,-R),K_R\right)^2
-\frac{R^2}{2},
\qquad
K_R=\left\{(y,s):s\ge\frac{p(y)}R\right\},
\tag{6}
\]
is globally convex, differentiable, and \(1\)-smooth, has minimum zero at \(x=0\), and its GD run with the original schedule satisfies
\[
\left|2F_R(x_n^{(R)})-2f(x_n)\right|\le\eta.
\tag{7}
\]
The polyhedron \(K_R\subset\mathbb R^{d+1}\) has at most \(n+2\) defining inequalities.

Here is the construction and its quantitative proof.

First restrict the original function to
\[
V=\operatorname{span}\{x_0,\nabla f(x_0),\ldots,\nabla f(x_{n-1})\}.
\]
Its dimension is at most \(n+1\). Every iterate belongs to \(V\), and every gradient used by the algorithm belongs to \(V\), so restricting \(f\) to this subspace preserves the entire run and its objective values. Convexity, smoothness, the minimizer, and the initial normalization are preserved.

Work henceforth in this subspace. For \(0\le i\le n\), write
\[
f_i=f(x_i),\qquad g_i=\nabla f(x_i),\qquad
y_i=x_i-g_i,\qquad
a_i=f_i-\frac12\|g_i\|^2.
\tag{8}
\]

We need the following inequality, which we derive rather than assume:
\[
f_i\ge
f_j+\langle g_j,x_i-x_j\rangle
+\frac12\|g_i-g_j\|^2.
\tag{9}
\]
The function \(z\mapsto f(z)-\langle g_j,z\rangle\) is convex and \(1\)-smooth and is minimized at \(x_j\). Applying the smoothness descent inequality at \(x_i\), with step equal to the negative gradient, proves (9).

Taking \(j\) to be the minimizer in (9) gives \(a_i\ge0\). Rearranging (9) also gives
\[
a_i\ge a_j+\langle g_j,y_i-y_j\rangle.
\tag{10}
\]

Now define
\[
p(y)=
\max\left\{
0,\;
a_i+\langle g_i,y-y_i\rangle:
0\le i\le n
\right\}.
\tag{11}
\]
Equation (10) implies
\[
p(y_i)=a_i,\qquad g_i\in\partial p(y_i).
\tag{12}
\]
Furthermore, (9) with the minimizer on its left gives
\[
a_i-\langle g_i,y_i\rangle
=f_i-\langle g_i,x_i\rangle+\frac12\|g_i\|^2
\le0.
\]
Thus \(p(0)=0\), as required.

Consider its quadratic envelope
\[
\bar f(x)=\min_y\left\{p(y)+\frac12\|x-y\|^2\right\}.
\tag{13}
\]
The minimizer is unique because the objective is strongly convex and coercive. If it is denoted by \(y(x)\), then
\[
\nabla\bar f(x)=x-y(x).
\tag{14}
\]
For completeness, the optimality condition is
\[
x-y(x)\in\partial p(y(x)).
\]
Monotonicity of the subdifferential, applied at two points, yields
\[
\|\nabla\bar f(x)-\nabla\bar f(z)\|^2
\le
\langle \nabla\bar f(x)-\nabla\bar f(z),x-z\rangle.
\tag{15}
\]
Comparing the objectives in (13) at their respective minimizers proves differentiability and (14); inequality (15) proves \(1\)-Lipschitz continuity of the gradient. Convexity follows from joint convexity of the minimized expression.

By (12), \(y_i\) is exactly the minimizer in (13) at \(x_i\). Consequently,
\[
\bar f(x_i)=f_i,\qquad
\nabla\bar f(x_i)=g_i.
\tag{16}
\]
Thus one static envelope already reproduces all the recorded values and gradients exactly.

We now realize an arbitrarily accurate version through the polyhedron in (6). Since \(p\ge0\), minimizing over its epigraph gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
F_R(x)
&=\min_y\left\{
\frac12\|x-y\|^2
+\frac12\left(R+\frac{p(y)}R\right)^2
-\frac{R^2}{2}
\right\}\\
&=\min_y\left\{
\frac12\|x-y\|^2+p(y)+\frac{p(y)^2}{2R^2}
\right\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{17}
\]
The function
\[
p_R=p+\frac{p^2}{2R^2}
\]
is convex because \(p\) is convex and nonnegative. The envelope argument just given therefore proves that \(F_R\) is globally convex and \(1\)-smooth. It is nonnegative and satisfies \(F_R(0)=0\).

At every original query point, evaluating (17) at \(y_i\) gives
\[
0\le F_R(x_i)-f_i\le\frac{a_i^2}{2R^2}.
\tag{18}
\]
Let \(y_i^{(R)}\) be the minimizer in (17). Strong convexity of
\[
y\mapsto p(y)+\frac12\|x_i-y\|^2
\]
and (18) imply
\[
\frac12\|y_i^{(R)}-y_i\|^2\le\frac{a_i^2}{2R^2}.
\]
Using the gradient formula,
\[
\|\nabla F_R(x_i)-g_i\|\le\frac{a_i}{R}.
\tag{19}
\]

To control the entire perturbed trajectory, define
\[
M(h)=\max\{1,|1-h|\}
\]
and
\[
A_0=0,\qquad
A_{t+1}=M(h_t)A_t+h_ta_t.
\tag{20}
\]
For any convex \(1\)-smooth function \(F\), the map \(I-h\nabla F\) is \(M(h)\)-Lipschitz for every \(h\ge0\). Indeed, setting
\[
u=x-z,\qquad v=\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(z),
\]
inequality (15), or the sum of two instances of (9), gives
\[
\langle u,v\rangle\ge\|v\|^2.
\]
Hence
\[
\|u-hv\|^2
\le\|u\|^2+h(h-2)\|v\|^2
\le M(h)^2\|u\|^2.
\tag{21}
\]

Start the \(F_R\)-run at the same \(x_0\). Equations (19)–(21) yield inductively
\[
\|x_t^{(R)}-x_t\|\le\frac{A_t}{R}.
\tag{22}
\]
Every quantity \(A_t\) is finite for every allowed schedule. No bounded-step assumption has entered.

Finally, smoothness, (18), (19), and (22) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
|F_R(x_n^{(R)})-f_n|
&\le
\frac{a_n^2}{2R^2}
+\left(\|g_n\|+\frac{a_n}{R}\right)\frac{A_n}{R}
+\frac{A_n^2}{2R^2}\\
&=
\frac{\|g_n\|A_n}{R}
+\frac{(a_n+A_n)^2}{2R^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{23}
\]
Thus the normalized performance error is bounded by
\[
\boxed{
|2F_R(x_n^{(R)})-2f_n|
\le
\frac{2\|g_n\|A_n}{R}
+\frac{(a_n+A_n)^2}{R^2}.
}
\tag{24}
\]
Choosing
\[
R\ge
\max\left\{
1,\,
\frac{4\|g_n\|A_n}{\eta},\,
\frac{\sqrt2\,(a_n+A_n)}{\sqrt\eta}
\right\}
\]
proves (7).

This proves, in particular, the exact identity
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal W_n(h)
=
\sup_{\substack{
1\le d\le n+1,\ R>0,\ \|x_0\|=1\\
p=\max(0,\ell_0,\ldots,\ell_n),\ p(0)=0
}}
2F_R(x_n^{(R)}).
}
\tag{25}
\]
One direction holds because every \(F_R\) is admissible. The other follows by approximating each admissible normalized run using (24). No uniform limit interchange is required.

The geometric distinction is essential. In (6), the algorithm operates on \(x\); the extra coordinate stays fixed at \(-R\). The minimizer used in the normalization is \(x_\star=0\), and the initial distance remains \(1\). Replacing that distance by the ambient distance to \((0,0)\), or updating the extra coordinate, would change the problem.

All steps above concern a single static globally smooth convex function. Zero steps cause no difficulty in (20), and arbitrarily large finite steps merely increase the explicitly tracked \(A_t\).

The unresolved theorem-level step is now precise. A positive resolution still requires selecting the affine functions in (5), and \(R\), from an arbitrary schedule so that
\[
2F_R(x_n^{(R)})\ge\frac{1-\varepsilon}{B_n}
\]
for every sufficiently large \(n\), with a threshold independent of the schedule. The construction above instead starts from an already existing adverse run; it does not produce that lower bound. Ordinary squared-distance constructions cannot fill this gap, by (1)–(4). No improving full-class upper certificate has been obtained either.
