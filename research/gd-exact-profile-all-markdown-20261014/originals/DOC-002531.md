## 5. Payment for EVERY finite directed interpolation row

Put z=||w||^2>0 and

    d=-<v,w>/z=2S/(1+4epsilon),
    u=w/sqrt(z), r_i=<u,x_i>, k_i=<u,g_i>.             (14)

Here u is a unit vector; d is an auxiliary scalar, not a physical step.
Since epsilon<=1/16 and S>=2,

    d>=8S/5, d-S>=3S/5>=6/5>1.                       (15)

By(6), the projected position increments and gradient increments are

    r_(i+1)-r_i=sqrt(z)*h_i*(d-P_i)>0,
    k_(i+1)-k_i=sqrt(z)*h_i>0.                        (16)

Define k(r) on[r_0,r_n] to be the continuous function affine on each
interval[r_i,r_(i+1)] and equal to k_i at r_i. Its slope on that
interval is exactly

    1/(d-P_i) <= ell:=1/(d-S)<1.                       (17)

Thus k is nondecreasing and ell-Lipschitz. This is the new available
payment. The longitudinal decrease in(5) supplies a finite scalar
curvature margin while the transverse component grows. No signed
quadratic cost is dropped or left to an unknown suffix budget.

The perpendicular component b=v-<u,v>*u of g_i is independent of i.
On a single interval, affine integration of k and(16) gives

    <b,x_(i+1)-x_i> + integral_(r_i)^(r_(i+1)) k(r)dr
       =<(g_i+g_(i+1))/2,x_(i+1)-x_i>
       =-(h_i/2)*<g_i+g_(i+1),g_i>.

Summing these EXACT equalities and using(9), for all i<j,

    f_j-f_i=<b,x_j-x_i>+integral_(r_i)^(r_j) k(r)dr.    (18)

Let Delta=k_j-k_i=sqrt(z)*(P_j-P_i)>0. Since k has Lipschitz
constant at most ell, r_j-r_i>=Delta/ell. On the final subinterval
of length Delta/ell one has
k(r)-k_i >= Delta-ell*(r_j-r); elsewhere k(r)-k_i>=0.
Consequently

    integral_(r_i)^(r_j)(k(r)-k_i)dr>=Delta^2/(2ell).

On the initial subinterval of the same length,
k_j-k(r)>=Delta-ell*(r-r_i), so also

    integral_(r_i)^(r_j)(k_j-k(r))dr>=Delta^2/(2ell).

The difference g_j-g_i is parallel to u and has norm Delta. Applying
(18) to these two area inequalities proves BOTH original residuals:

    Q_ij <= -(d-S-1)*z*(P_j-P_i)^2/2 <0,
    Q_ji <= -(d-S-1)*z*(P_j-P_i)^2/2 <0.              (19)

This proof covers every pair, however remote, and includes j=n. It is
not an adjacent-row test or a first-order Taylor expansion. Every term
of the finite-amplitude policy has been retained.
