## 2. Scalar identities and actual positive semidefinite geometry

Suppose the suffix has been constructed. Its points and gradients lie
in span{e_(i+1),...,e_n}. Put q=q_(i+1), delta=delta_(i+1), t=t_i,
d=d_i, h=1+d, and sigma=sigma_i. Then

    v_i^2=(1-t)[t q+(delta+q)/d]+sigma >=0.

The vectors in (1), not merely their pairwise proposed correlations,
therefore give a real positive semidefinite Gram matrix. Taking inner
products in x_i=x_(i+1)+h g_i gives

    delta_i = delta+(1+d t)q+d sigma,                         (2)
    Rtail_i_sq = Rtail_(i+1)_sq+h^2 q_i+2h t(delta+q),                     (3)

Here Rtail_i_sq=||x_i||^2; R_sq denotes a generic squared-radius state.
The fresh Rtail notation avoids the accepted ray channel's different r_i.
 These identities also prove delta_i>=0 and q_i>0
at every index. All dimensions are physical dimensions, not radii.

For i<j, orthogonality of the innovations gives

    <g_i,g_j>=(product_(k=i)^(j-1) t_k) q_j >=0.              (4)

Also q_i>=t_i q_(i+1). Repeated use of this inequality implies

    <g_i,g_j><=q_i  for every i<j.                            (5)

For the concrete all-t=beta_m pilot, 0<t_i<1 and v_i>0 at every
physical index. Its gradients are all distinct, and adjacent inner
products are strictly positive. This statement follows symbolically
from (1), without evaluating any instance.

## 3. Every original ordered row, including the remote rows

Let y_i=x_i-g_i. We prove that

    <y_i,g_j-g_i><=0 for every i,j in {0,...,n},
    <y_i,-g_i><=0.                                          (6)

The second inequality is -delta_i<=0. The adjacent forward comparison
has the exact value

    <y_i,g_(i+1)-g_i>
      =(1-t_i)(delta_(i+1)+q_(i+1))
        +d_i(t_i q_(i+1)-q_i)
      =-d_i sigma_i <=0.                                   (7)

For a remote j>i+1, use y_i=y_(i+1)+g_(i+1)+d_i g_i. The fresh
innovation is orthogonal to g_j and g_(i+1), so

    <y_i,g_j-g_(i+1)>
      =<y_(i+1),g_j-g_(i+1)>
       +(1+d_i t_i)<g_(i+1),g_j-g_(i+1)> <=0.                (8)

The first term is nonpositive by backward induction; the second is
nonpositive by (5). Adding (7) proves the remote comparison. At i=n
there is no future vertex.

For a past j<i, write

    g_j=(product_(k=j)^(i-1)t_k)g_i+w,
    w in span{e_j,...,e_(i-1)}.

Since y_i is orthogonal to w, the corresponding expression in (6) is
(product t_k-1)delta_i<=0. This also establishes all the past
comparisons at the terminal index. Thus (6) includes every ordered
pair, however remote or in different recursive children.

The maximizer defining F is P_C(x), the Euclidean projection onto C.
Indeed completing the square turns maximization into minimization of
||v-x||^2. The projection characterization (6) gives P_C(x_i)=g_i.
For completeness the two projection inequalities at arbitrary x,z
give

    <P_C(x)-P_C(z),x-z> >= ||P_C(x)-P_C(z)||^2.

Consequently P_C is 1-Lipschitz. Comparing the two maximizing affine
functions in the definition of F gives

    <P_C(x),u> <= F(x+u)-F(x) <= <P_C(x+u),u>.

The difference from the leftmost linear expression is between zero
and ||u||^2. Therefore F is differentiable, grad F=P_C, and its
gradient is 1-Lipschitz. F is finite because C is compact, and convex
because it is a supremum of affine functions of x.

The actual sampled values are

    f_i=F(x_i)=delta_i+q_i/2.                                (9)

Include x_*=g_*=f_*=0 and y_*=0. For the original convention in
1252b8823bb5a290, direct expansion gives the COMPLETE row identity

    Q_ij=f_i-f_j+<g_i,x_j-x_i>+||g_i-g_j||^2/2
        =<y_j,g_i-g_j> <=0.                                (10)

In particular Q_(i,*)=0 and Q_(*,j)=-delta_j<=0. No optimum row,
incoming terminal row, or cross-block row was relaxed.

## 5. Exact reduction of all continuous correlations to binary choices

Set sigma=0 for the rest of the optimization. At fixed eta the
terminal state is (eta^2,eta-1,1). Each map (1)--(3) is linear in the
suffix state and affine in its own t_i. Each parameter occurs in only
one map. Their composition Rtail_0_sq(t_0,...,t_(n-1)) is thus affine
separately in every t_i. Repeatedly using

    p(...,t_i,...)=(1-t_i)p(...,0,...)+t_i p(...,1,...)

gives the exact convex-combination identity

    Rtail_0_sq(t)=sum_(epsilon in {0,1}^n)
       [product_(epsilon_i=1)t_i product_(epsilon_i=0)(1-t_i)]
       Rtail_0_sq(epsilon).                                       (11)

The weights are nonnegative and sum to one. The numerator 2eta-1
is the same for every term. Therefore

    V(t,eta)<=max_(epsilon in {0,1}^n) V(epsilon,eta).         (12)

This is a mathematical vertex reduction, not an enumeration that was
run. It remains valid for horizon-dependent choices and after taking
the supremum over eta>=1. The fresh-vector square roots do not need
to be affine: it is the exact radius state (1)--(3) that is affine.

For a binary choice, t_i=1 repeats the same gradient. At t_i=0 the
current gradient is orthogonal to EVERY later gradient, with norm
q_i=(delta_(i+1)+q_(i+1))/d_i. Thus the binary choices partition the
word into constant-gradient phases separated by selected physical
checkpoint steps. Repeated physical queries remain in the word;
only the algebra for a phase is compressed.

Write the exact partition as

    (block_0,b_0,block_1,b_1,...,b_(k-1),block_k),

where s_j is the mass of block_j and d_j^cp=b_j-1>0. The superscript
cp distinguishes checkpoint offsets from the physical indexed d_i.
Define a paid scalar A by starting at A=0 and, for each checkpoint,
updating

    T=s_j+b_j,    A <- T(T+A)/d_j^cp.                        (13)

At the end let r=s_k and X=r+eta. The exact squared radius is

    Rtail_0_sq=X(X+A),   V=(2X-D)/[X(X+A)],  D=2r+1.                (14)

To verify (13), denote the mutually orthogonal phase gradients by
p_j and their squared norms by Q_j^phase. Put T_j=s_j+b_j for j<k
and T_k=X=s_k+eta. At the checkpoint ending phase j, its current
coordinate is b_j p_j and is extinguished by that step. Hence the
initial point is exactly sum_(j=0)^k T_j p_j. The norm recursion
in (1), evaluated at each checkpoint, is

    Q_j^phase=(T_(j+1)/d_j^cp)Q_(j+1)^phase,
    Q_k^phase=1.

For a nonterminal next phase this uses
delta_next+q_next=(s_(j+1)+b_(j+1))Q_(j+1)^phase;
for the final phase it uses delta_next+q_next=X. Define

    A_j=[sum_(ell<j)T_ell^2 Q_ell^phase]/[T_j Q_j^phase].

Then A_0=0 and
A_(j+1)=T_j(T_j+A_j)/d_j^cp by substitution of the norm ratio.
The complete squared radius is
sum_(j=0)^k T_j^2 Q_j^phase=X^2+A_k X, proving (13),(14).
For k=0, A=0 and X=sum h+eta, so the same formula applies.

The maximum over eta>=1 is attained at

    X_*=(D+sqrt(D^2+2AD))/2,
    V_* = 2/[A+D+sqrt(D^2+2AD)].                             (15)

Indeed differentiating (14) gives a numerator
-2X^2+2DX+AD, positive before X_* and negative after it.
Also X_*>=D>=r+1, so the maximizer lies in the permitted domain.

## 6. A local checkpoint-removal lemma with both boundary cases paid

Consider a checkpoint b=1+d with ordinary mass s immediately before
it, and put T=s+b. Let A>=0 be the accumulated paid state BEFORE this
phase. Including this checkpoint changes it to

    A_b=T(T+A)/d.

If a later checkpoint c exists, let r be the ordinary mass between
b and c, and X=r+c. At c, including b produces the numerator
X(X+A_b) in update (13); deleting b produces
(T+X)(T+X+A). Their exact difference is

    Delta = X[A_b-2T-A]-T(T+A)
          = T[X(T-2d)/d-T]
             +A[X(T-d)/d-T].                              (16)

If b is the last checkpoint, precisely the same difference is the
difference of the two final radii, now with X=r+eta.

If T>2d and

    X >= dT/(T-2d),                                       (17)

both brackets in (16) are nonnegative. The condition for the second
bracket is weaker because T-d>T-2d. Deleting b then cannot increase
the radius at that same eta. If c exists, its denominator c-1 is
unchanged, and every later update (13) is increasing in its input
A; thus this local comparison propagates to the actual terminal.

There is a separate last-checkpoint argument. Suppose

    s+1>=2d,   r+1>=2d.                                   (18)

Then T>=3d and D=2r+1>=2d. At the optimizer of the split word,
formula (15), with A_b in place of A, gives

    X_*>=sqrt(A_b D/2)>=sqrt(T^2 D/(2d))>=T.

Since T>=3d, one has T>=dT/(T-2d). Thus (17) holds at the
SPLIT word's optimizer. The merged word at this same eta has at
least the split word's optimized payoff. Its own optimized payoff
can only be larger. This proves the required comparison even though
deleting the last checkpoint need not improve radius at every eta.

    Rtail_split_sq-Rtail_merged_sq = T M(1/q-2-q).                        (24)

    d_2=lambda(1-1/m^2),
    M_2=lambda^2-1/m-4/[lambda(m+1)].

It does not contradict (T), which optimizes the terminal ratio.

Third, nonnegative backward coefficients larger than one do not
silently reopen this same geometry. In any legal realization with
the same fresh-innovation orthogonality and radial terminal point,
the reverse adjacent normal gives

    <y_(i+1),g_i-g_(i+1)>=(t_i-1)delta_(i+1)<=0.             (25)

For eta>1 every reserve is positive, forcing t_i<=1. For eta=1,

Rtail_(n-1)_sq. Keep all earlier t in [0,1] and their nonnegative sigma
as derived from their original forward adjacent inequalities.
The nonnegative state maps then reduce the initial radius as well.
The replacement is a member of (1). Therefore the same upper bound
also covers this exceptional nonnegative coefficient case. This is
an optional enlargement of the obstruction, not an assumption used
to construct the pilot.

The closure DOES NOT cover negative backward coefficients; a new
innovation correlated with a second future mode; arbitrary conjugate

moving transverse terminal position. It is not a closure of all
