## 1. Actual frame, primal metric, dual metric, and support arcs

Let e_0,...,e_k be an orthonormal basis and 0<=r<1. Write
s=sqrt(1-r^2), u_0=e_0 and u_(i+1)=-r*u_i+s*e_(i+1).
Here r is the task's r_corr, not a covariance law for queried gradients.
Induction, using the new coordinate e_(i+1), gives

    ||u_i||=1, <u_i,u_j>=(-r)^|i-j|.                     (1)

Thus, for arbitrary real coefficients a_i,

    ||sum_i a_i u_i||^2
      =sum_i a_i^2+2 sum_(i<j) (-r)^(j-i) a_i a_j.      (2)

The coefficients are NOT orthonormal coordinates. If b_i=<u_i,x>,
the orthonormal coordinates of x satisfy

    <e_0,x>=b_0,
    <e_i,x>=(b_i+r*b_(i-1))/s, i>=1,
    ||x||^2=b_0^2+sum_(i=1)^k (b_i+r*b_(i-1))^2/s^2. (3)

For k>=1 the inverse of the Gram matrix in (1) is tridiagonal:
its two end diagonal entries are 1/s^2, its interior diagonal entries
are (1+r^2)/s^2, and each adjacent off-diagonal entry is r/s^2.
This follows by expanding (3), since b=U^T x for U with columns u_i.
For k=0 both matrices are [1]. In particular constant positive inner
products b_i=d require EXACTLY

    ||x||^2=d^2[1+k*(1+r)/(1-r)].                       (4)

This is a conditional radius cost for that prescribed array, not a
claim that every correlated witness must pay it.

Absolute row sums of the Gram matrix and its inverse give

    (1-r)/(1+r)<=lambda_min(Gram)
       <=lambda_max(Gram)<=(1+r)/(1-r).                (5)

For fixed r and growing k both extremes are approached: insert the
constant and alternating-sign coefficient columns in (2), divide by
k+1, and sum the resulting geometric series. The Rayleigh quotients
tend (1-r)/(1+r) and (1+r)/(1-r), respectively. Consequently the
conditioning can approach ((1+r)/(1-r))^2. If k*(1-r) tends to0,
the alternating-sign Rayleigh quotient is (k+1)(1+o(1)); an interior
diagonal entry of the inverse also implies
lambda_min(Gram)<=s^2/(1+r^2) for k>=2. Both types of degeneration
are relevant, depending on the joint limit.

Fix epsilon>0 and define the endpoint and its actual Euclidean data by

    w_i=epsilon*u_i-u_(i+1)
       =(epsilon+r)*u_i-s*e_(i+1),
    omega=sqrt(1+epsilon^2+2*epsilon*r),
    theta=arctan(s/(epsilon+r)).                        (6)

In the ORTHONORMAL plane (u_i,e_(i+1)), put

    E_i(t)=cos(t)*u_i-sin(t)*e_(i+1),
    T_i(t)=-sin(t)*u_i-cos(t)*e_(i+1),
    c=omega*exp(kappa*theta),
    V_i(t)=c*exp(-kappa*t)*E_i(t), 0<=t<=theta,
    N_i(t)=E_i(t)+kappa*T_i(t), kappa>0.               (7)

The endpoints are c*u_i and w_i. If delta=z-t, then

    <N_i(t),V_i(z)>/[c*exp(-kappa*t)]
      =exp(-kappa*delta)*(cos(delta)+kappa*sin(delta)).

Its derivative in delta is
-(1+kappa^2)*exp(-kappa*delta)*sin(delta). As |delta|<pi/2,
the expression has its maximum1 at0; its inner product with the
additional slope0 is strictly smaller. Thus N_i(t) supports the
convex hull of0 and the whole arc at V_i(t). At t=0, e_(i+1) is
also a supporting normal. This verifies the actual Euclidean arc.

Writing V_i(t)=p*u_i-q*u_(i+1) gives

    p=c*exp(-kappa*t)*(cos(t)-r*sin(t)/s),
    q=c*exp(-kappa*t)*sin(t)/s,
    p>=epsilon*q>=0, p<=c.                            (8)

For t>0 the first inequality follows from cot(t)>=(epsilon+r)/s;
at t=0 it follows from p=c,q=0.
For two such slopes in consecutive stages one has EXACTLY

    <p*u_i-q*u_(i+1), p'*u_(i+1)-q'*u_(i+2)>
       =-(r*p+q)*(p'+r*q').                           (9)

For stage separation d>=1 the right side is multiplied by
(-r)^(d-1). This follows by substituting (1) into the four terms.
In particular adjacent slopes have negative inner products, while
slopes two stages apart have positive ones when r>0. No assertion
about Markov dependence of all gradients follows from (9).

### 3.5 Global smoothness, every directed row, and the exact radius

    ||g-g'||^2<=<g-g',x-x'>.

F(x+w)>=F(x)+<g,w>. These bounds prove differentiability with
gradient g, convexity, and a globally1-Lipschitz gradient.
The minimum is0 and argmin F={J=0}.

## 5. Local activation, transfer, radius, and turning costs

The following accounts for the apparent small-angle gain and its
limits. It does not turn the failed repeated rule into a lower bound.

For a general ordered nonnegative ordinary word, a backward arc
trajectory can be constructed in the actual orthonormal plane.
Start at w=(epsilon+r,-s). Suppose the current backward point is
(X,-Y), projected to V(theta_j), with positive X,Y. For a step alpha,
if

    Y<=kappa*[X+(alpha-1)*c],

the preceding projection is c*u and the preceding point is obtained
by adding alpha*c*u. Put mu=X+(alpha-1)*c; its normal is
mu*N(0)+(kappa*mu-Y)*e_new, with nonnegative coefficients.
Otherwise choose the unique angle t in (0,theta_j] for which

    <(X,-Y),E(t)-T(t)/kappa>/[c*exp(-kappa*t)]=1-alpha. (44)

The left side equals1 at theta_j. Its value at0 is
(X-Y/kappa)/c<1-alpha, and its derivative is
(kappa+1/kappa)*(X*cos(t)+Y*sin(t))/[c*exp(-kappa*t)]>0.
The root therefore exists uniquely. Resolving radial and tangent
components shows that adding alpha*V(t) gives a nonnegative
supporting normal at that slope. The tangent coefficient is
nonnegative because the polar angle of the current point is at
least theta_j and the new angle is no larger. Backward addition
preserves X>0,Y>0 and Y/X<=s/(epsilon+r). A capped point stays
capped in all earlier steps. These observations inductively prove
the asserted local construction, including alpha=0.

At an uncapped point use the representation R*((1+t)*E+kappa*t*T).
For two consecutive backward angles separated by Delta>=0, its
normal coefficient obeys exactly

    t_old=B(Delta)+A(Delta)*t_new,
